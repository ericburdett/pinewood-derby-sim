      Learn the physics

      A pinewood derby car has no motor — it's powered only by gravity. It trades the height it starts at for speed, then fights friction and air all the way to the finish. Below is what actually matters, ranked the way Mark Rober lab-tested it — and our simulator is tuned to match.

      What matters most — Mark Rober's order

      1. Weight, and putting it in the back (5 oz, ~1″ ahead of the rear axle) ~4.6 lengths
      2. Lighter wheels ~2.1 lengths
      3. Polished & bent axles (less friction) High
      4. Rail-riding (steer into one rail) Medium
      5. Aerodynamics (wedge body) ~1.4 lengths
      6. Three wheels touching ~1.1 lengths
      7. Graphite on the axles Helps #3

      The "lengths" are how far ahead each trick finishes, from Mark Rober's testing. Weight and where you put it dominate everything — nail those first, then work down the list.

      🏋️ Weight Huge

      What it is: the total weight of your car — most leagues cap it at 5.0 oz.

      The science: a heavier car stores more potential energy at the top of the ramp, and that turns into more speed. Get as close to the 5 oz limit as you can.

      See it: drop from 5 oz to 2 oz and the clock jumps almost half a second.

      ⚖️ Weight placement Huge

      What it is: where the weight sits. Rober's rule: about 1 inch ahead of the rear axle.

      The science: weight toward the back stays high on the ramp longer, so gravity pulls on it through more of the drop. Too far back, though, and the front goes light and wanders — there's a sweet spot.

      See it: the trickiest lever — small moves change a lot, and overshooting backfires.

      ⚙️ Lighter wheels ~2.1 lengths

      What it is: how much each wheel weighs.

      The science: a spinning wheel stores energy too (rotational inertia). Lighter wheels spin up easier, so more of gravity's energy goes into moving forward instead of into spinning — Rober's #2 trick.

      See it: slide wheel weight down and the clock drops.

      🛢️ Axle prep: polish, bend & graphite High

      What it is: how slippery the axles are where the wheels spin.

      The science: polishing the axles, gently bending them so the wheel rides smoothly against the nail head, and adding graphite all cut friction — the speed-stealer that acts the whole way down.

      See it: slide axle prep from polished toward bare metal and you lose a couple tenths.

      🛤️ Rail-riding (steering) Medium

      What it is: a tiny steer so the car leans against one guide rail instead of bouncing between both.

      The science: no steer → it ping-pongs and every bump steals speed; a few degrees pins it to one rail so it rolls straight; too much scrubs. It's a valley — a little is best.

      See it: sweep the steering slider for the fastest spot, around 3°.

      💨 Aerodynamics ~1.4 lengths

      What it is: how much air the car pushes — its shape (sleek wedge vs. blocky) and frontal size (width × height).

      The science: a low, sleek wedge slips through the air while a tall blocky brick plows it. It matters less than weight or friction, but Rober still measured about 1.4 car lengths — so don't ignore it.

      See it: turn the body into a tall, wide brick and watch the time slip.

      🛞 Three wheels touching ~1.1 lengths

      What it is: lifting one front wheel a hair so only three touch the track.

      The science: a wheel that isn't touching can't add friction or steal spin energy — so three rolling wheels beat four. (Check your pack allows it.)

      See it: switch "Wheels touching" to 3 and the clock ticks down a little.

      📏 Size & the rule book Legality only

      What it is: wheelbase and the body's length/width/height don't set speed directly — they decide whether your car is legal.

      The science: packs cap weight at 5 oz and limit the car's size so the race is fair. The Inspection panel checks these live as you build.

      See it: push a dimension past its limit and the inspection flags it.
